# Sequence 1 — kit delivery and nurture

Fires when someone submits the form on the playbook page. Five emails over 14 days.

**Goal:** deliver what was promised, earn the right to be in their inbox, and get the person to
generate one book. Not to sell — the sale happens after the product has been seen.

**Tooling note:** Web3Forms drops these in your inbox but cannot send sequences. For the first
~100 subscribers, send emails 1 and 2 manually — it is worth it, and Rob's advice with a small
list is to DM every single person anyway. Past that, move the list to Beehiiv or Loops and
automate the rest.

---

## Email 1 — immediate

**Subject:** Your operator kit

> Here it is: **drafttodone.io/kit**
>
> Four documents. The checklist is the one that matters most — it's the one that would have
> saved my account.
>
> Quick context on why I wrote these. I ran a KDP catalog to €400/month and lost the whole
> account overnight, over a pen name that was a pun. Every title, every review, every ranking.
> Not suspended. Terminated.
>
> Everything in the kit is what I do differently now.
>
> One ask, and it's genuinely an ask rather than a funnel: **reply and tell me how many books
> you've published.** I answer every one myself, and knowing where you are changes what I send
> you next.
>
> — Antoine

**Why:** deliver first, no pitch, and a reply request. Replies teach the inbox provider that
this address is wanted, and every reply is a customer conversation Rob would tell you to be
having anyway.

## Email 2 — day 2

**Subject:** the pen name that cost me the account

> Yesterday I sent you the kit. Today the story behind it, because the checklist makes more
> sense once you know what it's protecting against.
>
> I was making about €400/month. Not life-changing. It was real and it was growing.
>
> One morning the account was gone.
>
> The reason took me weeks to piece together: one of my pen names was a pun. Funny to me. To
> the review system it read as an attempt to mislead readers about who wrote the book.
>
> Three things I learned that aren't obvious until it happens:
>
> **Termination is account-level, not title-level.** I'd assumed a flagged book costs you that
> book. It cost me the catalog, including the titles that were selling fine.
>
> **The appeal is not a conversation.** A form, then a templated reply. Plan as if there is no
> appeal.
>
> **I'd worried about entirely the wrong things.** I was careful about content quality and
> completely careless about author identity.
>
> Section 1 of the checklist is compliance for exactly this reason. It's the section people
> skip, and it's the one that decides whether the catalog exists in a year.
>
> — Antoine

## Email 3 — day 5

**Subject:** it isn't the algorithm

> Something you can check in your own dashboard in about ninety seconds.
>
> Open KDP, go to reports, find impressions.
>
> Take a book that sold four copies last month and look at its impression count. It'll be in
> the thousands.
>
> Amazon showed that book to thousands of people. It wasn't buried. Almost everyone who saw it
> scrolled past.
>
> That's a completely different problem — and much better news, because suppression isn't
> fixable and packaging is.
>
> The metadata worksheet in the kit is the fix. The short version:
>
> - **Title carries the promise. Subtitle carries the keywords.** Most people make the title do
>   both and it does neither well.
> - **The 7 slots are phrases, not words.** "sourdough" is a word. "why is my sourdough flat" is
>   what somebody typed.
> - **The first two lines of the description** are all most buyers read. Put the outcome there,
>   not your credentials.
>
> Try one thing: change only the subtitle on one book. Leave everything else alone. Check in 30 days.
>
> — Antoine

---

## Email 4 — day 9

**Subject:** most of my books lost money

> Everyone posts their winners, so here's the whole sheet.
>
> The cost nobody counts is your own time. Generation is cheap. Review, metadata and upload took
> me about 90 minutes a book — which at any reasonable hourly rate is more than the generation cost.
>
> Once I counted that, the number that mattered turned out to be **14**. That's how many copies
> a book had to sell before it was worth having made.
>
> Not "will this sell." **Will this sell fourteen copies.** That reframed every niche decision
> I made afterwards.
>
> The distribution was brutal and I suspect it's normal: most books never hit 14, a few broke
> even and stalled, and a small number carried everything.
>
> The most expensive mistake in the sheet wasn't a bad book. It was staying in one niche for
> seven books because I liked the topic. **Three books that go nowhere is the signal to leave.**
>
> The economics sheet is in the kit. Run your own numbers — I'd genuinely like to know whether
> your break-even number is close to 14.
>
> — Antoine

## Email 5 — day 14

**Subject:** want me to run one for you?

> Last one in this sequence, and it's an offer with nothing attached.
>
> Reply with a niche and I'll generate the complete package — manuscript, full wrap cover,
> title, subtitle, description, all 7 keyword slots, pen name — and send it back to you.
>
> Free. No signup, nothing to cancel. Yours to publish if it's good.
>
> I'm doing this because I want to know where it breaks before I sell more of it, and because
> the fastest way for you to judge the thing is to look at one.
>
> One condition, and it's the honest one: **run the checklist on it before you upload.** Read
> the book. The tool does the mechanical part well and it does not replace your judgement.
>
> If you'd rather run it yourself: **app.drafttodone.io**
>
> Either way, thanks for reading this far.
>
> — Antoine

**Why this closes the sequence:** it converts a reader into a conversation, which is the step
that produces both customers and the feedback that fixes the product. It also mirrors the free
generation play from Phase 1, so the same asset serves both channels.

## After the sequence

Move them to the weekly list. **One to three emails a week**, and the content is already
written in [`SOCIAL-POSTS.md`](../content/SOCIAL-POSTS.md) — the value posts work almost
unchanged as short emails.

Rule: never let the list go cold and then reappear with a launch. That is how open rates die.
