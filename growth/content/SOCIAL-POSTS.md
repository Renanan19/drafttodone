# X and LinkedIn post bank

Rob Hoffman's hook formula runs through all of these: **social proof + curiosity + promised
benefit**. And his rule — *"stop creating selfish content"* — is why almost none of these are
about DraftToDone.

**Cadence:** daily. The founder posts; there is no team.
**Split:** roughly 8 value posts to 1 product post to 1 personal post.
**Platform note:** on LinkedIn, publish the long ones as **articles**, not posts — LinkedIn
articles are indexed by Google, and answer engines read Google.

---

## The lead-magnet format (the highest-converting one)

Jedi: half his posts using this format cleared 100K views. The mechanic is that comments drive
reach, so you ask for a comment rather than a click.

> Amazon banned my KDP account overnight over a pen name.
>
> €400/month gone. Every title, every review, every ranking.
>
> Since rebuilding I run a 6-part checklist before every upload. It has caught things that
> would have cost me the account a second time.
>
> Comment "CHECKLIST" and I'll send it. Free, no signup.

Variants of the same mechanic:

> I've generated over N books with AI. About X of them were publishable without edits.
>
> Here's the 6-point teardown of what breaks in a long generated manuscript, and how long each
> fix actually takes.
>
> Comment "TEARDOWN".

> Most KDP books lose money once you count your own time.
>
> I built a sheet that shows exactly how many copies a book has to sell before it's worth
> having made. For me the number was 14.
>
> Comment "SHEET" and it's yours.

**Automation:** Tweet Hunter has a native lead-magnet DM trigger on X (official API, no ban
risk). ManyChat does it on Instagram. On LinkedIn it can be manual until volume forces the issue.

## Value posts — the ban and compliance

> The mistake that cost me my KDP account wasn't content quality.
>
> It was a pen name that was a pun.
>
> Funny to me. To the review system it read as an attempt to mislead readers about who wrote
> the book. Account terminated, whole catalog gone.
>
> Your pen name has to read like a person who could exist. That's the whole rule.

> Three things that get a KDP account terminated in 2026, in the order people actually hit them:
>
> 1. Undisclosed AI content
> 2. Exceeding the daily title limit
> 3. A pen name that reads as misleading
>
> Note what's not on the list: using AI. Declaring it is not a penalty. Not declaring it is.

> Termination on KDP is account-level, not title-level.
>
> Most people plan as though a bad book costs them that book.
>
> It can cost you the catalog. Including the titles that were selling.

> The appeal process is not a conversation.
>
> You get a form. You get a templated reply.
>
> Plan as if there is no appeal, because functionally there isn't one.

## Value posts — metadata

> Your KDP books aren't selling and it probably isn't the algorithm.
>
> Go into your dashboard and look at impressions. If a book got 30 sales and 4,000 impressions,
> Amazon showed it 4,000 times and 3,970 people didn't click.
>
> That's not suppression. That's a packaging problem, and packaging is fixable.

> The title carries the promise. The subtitle carries the keywords.
>
> Most operators make the title do both, and it does neither well.
>
> "Sourdough Baking" is a topic. "Why Your Sourdough Is Flat" is what someone typed.

> The 7 backend keyword slots are phrases, not words.
>
> "sourdough" is a word. "why is my sourdough flat" is a phrase a buyer typed.
>
> If a word is already in your title, it's already indexed. Repeating it in a slot buys nothing
> and burns a slot.

> Where to find real keywords, free, in 5 minutes:
>
> Open Amazon. Start typing the job your book solves.
>
> The autocomplete is real search behaviour, from real buyers, updated continuously. It's better
> than most paid tools and it costs nothing.

> Most buyers read two lines of your description and decide.
>
> So don't spend those two lines on your credentials.
>
> Put the outcome there. In their words, not yours.

> Ranking in a category you can win beats invisibility in one you can't.
>
> Open the category. Look at the current #10 book. Can yours beat it?
>
> If not, go narrower. Narrower is not smaller — it's winnable.

## Value posts — niche selection

> The 3-star reviews of the top books in your niche are writing your next book for you.
>
> 3-star reviewers finished the book and still felt something was missing.
>
> They will tell you exactly what it was.

> Check the date of the most recent review before you enter a niche.
>
> Recent reviews mean recent buyers.
>
> A category where the top titles have no reviews in six months has stopped buying, whatever
> the search volume says.

> High search volume plus established competitors is the worst possible place to start.
>
> Everyone picks niches on volume. Volume is the last thing to look at.

> Kill a niche after three books that go nowhere.
>
> I stayed in one for seven because I liked the topic.
>
> That was the single most expensive decision in my spreadsheet.

> When a book works, make the next three adjacent to it.
>
> Adjacent beats novel. Every time.

## Value posts — the honest AI take

These do double duty: they build trust and they position the product correctly.

> AI didn't remove the work of publishing a book.
>
> It moved it. From writing to editing.
>
> That's faster. It is not zero, and anyone selling you zero is selling you an account
> termination.

> If you published 20 AI-generated books last month without reading them:
>
> Some of them contain a fabricated statistic.
>
> You don't know which ones. That's the actual problem.

> The failure mode of AI manuscripts nobody warns you about: a confident, specific,
> completely invented number with a plausible-sounding source.
>
> If it names a figure, verify it or cut it. There's no third option.

> Volume is a production capability. It should not be your promise.
>
> If your plan is "publish as many books as possible", you're optimising for exactly the
> behaviour Amazon is currently suppressing.

## Building-in-public posts

Florian's own move: document the journey, and the documentation becomes the distribution.

> Public goal: €0 → €3,000 MRR in 90 days.
>
> Starting position: 2 customers, no email list, an over-built SEO site and a product I'm
> confident in.
>
> I'll post the number every week whether it's up or not.

> Week N: [number]. [One thing that worked.] [One thing that didn't.]
>
> [The specific number that moved.]

> I spent months building 2,400 SEO pages before I had customers.
>
> That was backwards. SEO compounds — but it compounds on something you've already validated.
>
> If I started again I'd do direct outreach first and build the SEO base with the proceeds.

> Offered to generate a complete KDP book package for free for anyone who commented a niche.
>
> [N] people did. Here's what I learned from the [N] conversations that followed.

## Product posts (roughly 1 in 10)

Subtle placement, never a hard pitch.

> Someone commented "a 30-day guide for short-term rental hosts who want fewer bad reviews."
>
> Here's the complete package that came out: manuscript, full wrap cover, title, subtitle,
> description, 7 keyword slots, pen name.
>
> [screenshots]
>
> Comment a niche and I'll run yours.

> The part of publishing that's mechanical: metadata and production.
> The part that isn't: choosing the niche, reviewing the book, deciding when to quit one.
>
> I automated the first. The second is still on you, and it should be.

## What not to post

- Anything that implies you can publish unlimited books safely. You cannot, and you know it.
- Fabricated MRR screenshots or invented customer counts. The number is 2 until it isn't.
- Engagement bait with no payload — it reaches people who will never buy.
- Anything about your morning routine. Nobody cares, and Rob's co-founder is right that this is
  the most common mistake founders make on these platforms.
