# Agent findings D — 8 Darroman episodes

Mined for tactics **not already in the known list** and plausibly usable by a €0-MRR,
product-led, prosumer SaaS. Contradictions are flagged `[CONTRADICTS]`. Things that only work
for B2C mobile are flagged `[B2C ONLY]`.

## 1. David — Stopper (quit-sugar iOS app, $13K/month peak), Soreal, WhatsTheApp

**Influencer contract mechanic — the guaranteed-views clause.** $1,000/month for 4 videos,
**20% paid upfront**, with a contractual **minimum 1,000,000 cumulative views across the 4
videos**. If the total isn't hit one week after video 4, the creator keeps posting for free
until it is. This is a real, copyable term sheet and it de-risks the whole first creator deal.
Directly upgrades the known creator ladder: the sub-10K commission tier can carry the same
cumulative-views floor instead of pure commission.

**Select creators by average views, not followers.** He targeted ~200K-follower accounts but the
actual filter was ~100K average views per video. Follower count was never the criterion.

**Outreach volume is brutal and the reply rate is the number to plan against.** ~100 creators
contacted per batch, across email + IG DM + TikTok DM + every link in their Linktree.
**2–3 replies out of 100 counts as a good batch.** Plan the KDP-creator outreach at that
conversion, not at 10%.

**Views do not equal downloads and he could not attribute.** Creators doing millions of views
brought him almost nothing; creators doing 50K views with lots of "what's the app?" comments
brought real installs. **Comment intent was the signal, not view count.** He tried coupon codes
and affiliate links — "people don't use it much." Back-of-napkin: match a download spike to the
video that was live.

**The revenue is rented, not owned.** "Whenever I stop marketing for Stopper, the revenue drops."
Influencer-driven revenue does not compound and stops the day payments stop. He now believes paid
ads would have been cheaper and steadier than influencers for the same app. Relevant caution
before committing budget to the creator ladder as the primary channel.

**Trend-jacking micro-sites, with numbers.** Peter Levels shipped TrustMRR (Stripe-verified
revenue) days after a Stripe announcement and made ~$18K in ~3 days. David copied the *shape* into
an adjacent vertical — same idea on RevenueCat instead of Stripe — as WhatsTheApp. **MVP built in
1 day.** At 7 days old: ~1,500 visitors/day, sold **3 ad placements at $500 each**. Soreal took 5
days to build. The transferable move is not the app, it's the pattern: when a KDP/Amazon policy or
tooling story breaks, ship a one-day free micro-tool for KDP operators and let it carry the brand.

**Build-in-public is a recruiting channel, not just a marketing one.** One tweet saying "looking
for a crack dev" → **50+ DMs in a day**, several from people already at $30K/month with their own
apps. He sourced a co-founder for two products this way. Cheapest possible way to find a
collaborator or an ops hire.

`[CONTRADICTS]` **Peter Levels' "don't share revenue, keep 100%."** Both David and Florian argue
against it: split 50/50 with someone who covers your weak side and you make 10x what you'd make
solo. Florian: "If I had to build the product, do the distribution and everything myself, I'd make
maybe 5K a month."

`[CONTRADICTS]` **Levels' "posting doesn't drive sales."** David tracks it directly: every post
that clears 10–15K views produces 1–2 sales. His read on why Levels believes otherwise: Levels'
products now have years of SEO traction, so he no longer *needs* the audience — but he'd have quit
those products before the SEO landed without early Twitter validation.

**Onboarding-as-sales-letter, and the research method behind it.** `[B2C ONLY]` for the
mechanics: 45 screens (Yazio runs 72), one sentence per screen, benefits and pain points repeated
across many screens. 75% of installs reach the paywall; ~4% convert. Do not port 45 screens to a
prosumer SaaS.
**Transferable part:** the copy research. He went to TikTok, watched what creators in the niche
said the pain points were, and used that exact vocabulary. For Stopper the winning angles were
"feel closer to God," "glowing skin" — not the obvious ones. The DraftToDone version: watch the
KDP YouTube/TikTok channels and lift the operator's literal vocabulary before writing any copy.

**Postbridge** — record once, publish to IG/TikTok/X/LinkedIn/YouTube in one click. Florian used
it to post daily for 85 days. See episode 5 for the important caveat.

`[B2C ONLY]`, noted and dismissed: 70% churn as acceptable; weirder concept = more viral; the
in-app Telegram community (17,000 people) being the actual retention driver rather than the
product. The one idea worth stealing from that last point is that a **peer community can be the
retention mechanism** — a KDP-operator Discord attached to the subscription.

## 2. Roman — GojiBerry ($300K MRR / $3.5M/yr, YC batch)

**The channel-per-stage ladder, with the actual revenue bands.** This is the most useful sequencing
data in the eight episodes:

| Band | Channel |
|---|---|
| $0 → $6K | outbound only |
| $10K → $25K | Reddit, almost exclusively |
| $25K → $75K | content + free blueprints |
| $75K → $150K | partnerships, X (3 separate founder accounts), B2B influencers, sponsored newsletters, lifetime affiliate program |
| $250K+ | **cut Reddit entirely** |

Nothing was dropped as he added — until Reddit, which he killed at ~$250K because (a) they'd
spammed hard enough to generate real hate and (b) the opportunity cost stopped making sense. By
then users were posting about GojiBerry on Reddit unprompted.

**Lifetime affiliate program** — called out specifically as the thing that "helped like crazy."
Lifetime commission, not 12-month. Zero cash cost at €0 MRR and it is the one lever a broke
founder can pull immediately.

`[CONTRADICTS] the "niche down hard" rule — the single highest-value item in these eight
episodes.` Roman's biggest growth lever was deliberately targeting the **adjacent AI niche rather
than the core niche**. His exact reasoning: "reaching people that do SEO is a niche everyone is
targeting. *How to improve your SEO thanks to AI* is a new niche." AI-framed content gets more
reach because Claude/OpenAI are pouring budget into that category and creators know AI topics
out-reach vertical topics. Result: "the distribution is going to 10x."
The stated cost is explicit and he does not hide it: **broader niche → higher churn**, and it
attracts individuals and small teams rather than the up-market accounts you'd want. He accepts it
because volume compensates.
For DraftToDone the concrete test is cheap: keep the ICP locked to the KDP catalog operator in the
*product and onboarding*, but write the top-of-funnel content as "how AI agents handle Amazon KDP
metadata / compliance / catalog ops" rather than "KDP tool." Same customer, 10x the reach of the
framing. He also confirms Neville and others ran the same landing-page swap toward AI-agent
language.

`[CONTRADICTS] "UGC does not work for B2B."` Roman runs UGC ads for a B2B SaaS at $300K MRR, via
an agency, and it is one of his three go-forward channels. The blueprint he copies is from
Nexfield/Arcade/Lovable: **post organically on TikTok and Instagram, wait until a piece goes viral
on its own, and only then put paid spend behind that specific piece.** No cold creative. That
distinction is probably why the known list has UGC failing for B2B — running paid on unproven
creative is what fails.

**Paid X viral launch, with prices.** Look at who quote-retweeted the biggest recent SaaS launches
(Nexfield, Lovable). Those are the paid accounts. DM them and pay them to quote-retweet yours.
**$1,000–$3,000 buys a viral X video.** Cheap enough to be a one-shot launch tactic later, not now.

**Delegation model for a solo growth operator.** Test each channel personally → write the
blueprint → hand the blueprint to a specialist agency and make them follow it. He uses separate
agencies for B2B influencers, SEO, UGC, and content; outbound is the one thing kept in-house (one
intern). He is a single person managing five agencies.
Founder-led content is the exception that cannot be delegated: his content agency's posts get
mostly rejected — he mines their hooks and angles and rewrites. "It's very hard for an agency to
know what's happening inside the brain of a founder."

**He refuses to give advice.** "I'm not telling people you should do this. I'm telling them this
is what I do, it works for me." Named Thibaut (Outrank/Revive) as running completely different
blueprints that work better for some products. Worth internalising before copying any of this
wholesale.

**YC perks, for calibration only:** ~$1M OpenAI credits, $300K Gemini, $300K Claude, plus AWS/Azure.
Raised at demo day, still own ~85%, no plans to raise again.

## 3. Jackie Chou — Indexy / LocalRank / Trackings ($374K last month, 13 income streams)

Densest episode of the eight. Several items are gray-hat; flagged.

**The reciprocal listicle ring — the best AEO tactic here.** Get **~20 indie founders in
non-competing niches** together. Each one publishes "best [X] 2026" listicles on their own blog,
placing the others at **#1**. Consistent #1 placement across many independent listicles is a
strong citation signal to LLMs. His framing: **"2026 is the year of the listicle."** This is a
sharper, cheaper version of the known GEO-listicle play — it's a swap ring, not a solo publishing
effort, and it needs 19 other founders, not budget. Won't work for niches too small to have 20
adjacent operators; KDP tooling is probably wide enough.

**You do not need Google rankings to be cited by ChatGPT.** A study of ~35K cited URLs found
roughly **50% of cited domains have zero traffic.** Google AI Mode is the opposite — it leans on
the Google ranking algorithm, so there you do need position. Practical split: for ChatGPT/Claude
citations, publish volume anywhere; for AI Overviews, you still need to rank.
Corroborated by episode 6 — Distribute got into a Google AI Overview inside a week.

**The Reddit SOP, step by step.** `[GRAY HAT — see warning]`
1. Google your target keyword as "best [X]". ~90% of the time a Reddit thread is ranking.
2. Go into that thread and comment your brand.
3. Wait two weeks.
4. Buy upvotes on your comment. Traffic starts immediately.
5. If the thread is locked: post your own question in the same subreddit ("what's the best X in
   2026?"), don't shill anything, buy ~500 upvotes so it hits the front page and displaces the old
   thread, then **edit your own post**: "Edit: actually I found X to be the best."

Prices he quoted: **~$0.01/upvote at his own service, ~$0.10 elsewhere** — 500 upvotes is $5–$50.
His claim about why it works: upvotes flip the narrative on a post that's getting hate, because
Redditors behave as followers.
**Warning, and it is not a small one.** This is a Reddit ToS violation and vote manipulation. The
founder here has already been permanently banned from one platform. Steps 1–3 (find the ranking
thread, leave a genuinely useful comment) are the safe 80% of the value. Step 4 onward is not
worth a second ban. Note also that Roman (ep. 2) killed Reddit at $250K MRR partly because the
spam generated real hostility toward his brand.

**Chrome-signal ranking manipulation.** `[GRAY HAT]` A page sitting at position 5–20 can be pushed
up by blasting real human traffic at it (he'd buy 50K hits from Facebook), because Google weights
Chrome user data. It holds after you stop paying only if natural CTR is real; if the page is bad
and people pogo-stick back, it tanks. Included for completeness — the legitimate read is that
**real clicks and low pogo-sticking are ranking factors**, so a page that actually satisfies the
query holds its position.

**VSL on the landing page.** A/B tested with and without on a B2B SaaS: having it wins. Two
minutes. Do **not** center it as a ClickFunnels-style VSL-plus-button page — headline first, top
of the video visible below the fold line so people can choose to watch. Stat he cites: viewers who
reach **90% of the VSL convert at ~60%.** He writes the script with ChatGPT. Caveat: he thinks
VSLs are a North-America-specific phenomenon and doesn't see them working on Canadian or European
audiences — worth knowing given DraftToDone's likely US-heavy KDP audience.

`[CONTRADICTS] the X-first cadence — with hard attribution.` LocalRank's launch did **$20K out of
the gate. 90% of that revenue came from YouTube; 10% from X.** The YouTube launch video did ~1,000
views. The X launch tweet did ~50,000 views. **Attributed via coupon codes, not guessed.** His
line: "You can shitpost all you want on X, but I'll take the 1K views on YouTube any day."
His reasoning on why 1,000 views is not small: the same 1,000 people return daily, they are US-based
with buying power, and most of them are agency owners. This is the second independent source in
this batch saying the same thing (see ep. 4, ep. 5).

**Format that worked for him:** daily YouTube video, deliberately *low* production — long,
unedited, no quick cuts, Sam Sulek style — precisely because everyone else in the space was doing
high-production quick-cut videos. First video did 100 views. Prep now takes 20 minutes; it took 2
hours for the first one.

**The SaaS playbook he now repeats.** Have genuine deep operator knowledge of an industry → notice
what's broken → build software for it → market it to the audience you built *by talking about that
exact problem*. "If you solve that with software, 90% of the time other people will have that
issue as well." Antoine ran a KDP catalog to €400/month and was permanently banned over a pen name.
That is exactly the operator scar tissue this playbook runs on, and it is currently not the
headline of anything.

**Customer-type economics.** Indexy's LTV on agency-owner customers is **~$1,000** — massive
switching costs, easiest customers to keep happy, one contact instead of fifty. Supports pricing
DraftToDone toward the catalog operator (multi-title, repeat) rather than the one-book author.

**Delegation heuristic:** anything you do more than once, delegate. And accept it: "the tasks you
delegate will never be executed up to your standards. It'll never be up to code. It just needs to
be good enough." He runs 13 income streams with 13 people.

**Community is his favorite of the 13 businesses** — easiest to run, and the one he'd keep if he
could keep only one, because it's where he actually gets his ideas ("we're coming up with ideas on
how to game stuff"). Directly relevant: a paid KDP-operator community is both a product and the
research channel that feeds the roadmap.

**The 90-day $0 → $10K plan he'd run himself.** Cold email every day + cold call every day + 2
TikToks + 2 IG reels + 1 long-form video + 1 X post, daily, no exceptions. High-ticket
**performance-based no-brainer offer** ("if you don't hit these parameters, you don't pay").
Expected outcome for an average person brute-forcing it: mid four figures/month; $10K if you know
what you're doing. Note he says do this as an **agency**, not a SaaS — SaaS is what you build after
the agency has taught you where the hole is.

**Diversification as insurance, with a number.** 99% of the people who piled into content sites
during COVID are down ~90% in enterprise value because they never took money off the table. His own
revenue fell **from $500K/month to ~$150–180K in 3 months** — a 60% haircut requiring team
restructuring and line-by-line credit-card cost cutting.

**Sprint hard when a channel actually opens.** He went from $70–80K/month to $500K/month **in
three months** when he found partnership SEO. "If you see an opportunity you should really go in
hard and nothing else should matter." This is the counterweight to the guardrails below — the
sustainable default is not sustainable *all* the time, but the sprint should be triggered by
evidence, not by a calendar.

**Reality check on gross revenue:** $374K gross → ~40% net → less after tax. Ten years in,
~$10M net worth.

## 4. Ole — "The AI Solopreneur" (7 figures in 18 months, then stopped) — SUSTAINABILITY

The requested episode. Concrete warnings only.

**The cadence that produced the growth, so the risk is priced correctly.** 0 → 100K followers in
**65 days** on X. The mechanism: pick a trending topic that isn't saturated yet (AI, Nov 2022) and
niche it one level down to a specific person (AI **for solopreneurs**). Find one format that
works. Then **7–8 posts a week of the same format** while everyone else did 2–3. Hate comments in
the replies were a feature — broad engagement, and the thread blew up anyway.

**The physical warning sign, verbatim.** Unexplained health issues first, then: **all the tendons
in both arms severely inflamed — he could not use his hands.** He describes it as chronic pain,
which "starts in the brain, not the tissue" — a nervous system stuck in an alarm cycle. His read
at the time: "I'm going to paralyse your arms until you stop what you're doing." For a founder
about to commit to 90 days of daily recording, editing and typing, hands/forearms/wrists are the
specific thing to watch.

**The trap is that it was working, not that it was failing.** "If you have something that works,
it's actually much harder than something that doesn't work. Because your whole external world will
tell you, this is amazing, keep doing it." Success removes the exit. There is no natural stopping
signal in a channel that is performing.

**Structural warning specific to a personal-brand cadence: it cannot be delegated or sold.** "For
most personal brand stuff it's actually impossible to get someone else in. So you have to kill it
if you don't want to do it." A 90-day daily-posting plan builds an asset only Antoine can operate,
forever. Anything that can be built alongside it and *is* transferable — the email list, the SEO
base, the product — is worth more per hour spent.

**The identity test, and it's a real test.** He built a second persona, "the longevity dad," which
was working. A follower DM'd him: "I don't believe you when you pretend to be this health pope." He
agreed instantly. The check: can you embody this positioning for five years without it becoming
someone you don't want to be? He warns how fast it happens — "four, five, ten years have gone by
and you're still the SEO guy," to the point where he now refuses income that isn't aligned.

**The concrete guardrail he actually adopted:** he will not vlog daily. **One vlog day per week**,
recording commentary as he goes. Reason: daily vlogging turns your whole life into "could this be
a vlog?" and imposes constraints (light, timing, location) on ordinary life. Weekly keeps the
format from colonising the days it isn't on.

**The asset that survived the burnout was the email list.** He funneled X → email **from week one**
and calls it "the most important part to make money, especially if you're on X." 40,000+ subscribers
survived the pivot away from the entire AI-guy identity, and he kept selling old products with no
new production. The list is the only thing on the plan that keeps paying if the cadence stops.

`[CONTRADICTS] X as the growth channel.` "X is not the best channel to grow your business at all.
It's more a relationship / authority / influence thing than a business-building platform. **It's
better to find people to help you build your business or to hire on X than it is to get leads.**"
He is moving to YouTube as the long-term business engine. Third independent source in this batch.

**Accountability hack that cost money on purpose.** He'd wanted to make his first YouTube video for
two years. He broke the block by **paying a consultant for a strategy he didn't need**, purely so
that not shipping would be self-evidently bullshitting himself. Cheap version: prepay something
non-refundable tied to the deliverable.

**Weekly content data ritual — three numbers, not vanity.** Rank posts by likes. Then look at
**which post drove the most profile clicks** (best proxy for follower conversion). Then look at
click-through and conversion on any lead magnet or newsletter plug. Iterate on the winner, keep the
format, understand *why* it won.

**Accidental pricing finding.** He added an expensive upsell only to create price contrast on the
low tier — "no one's going to buy it." **Eight people bought it.** A deliberate high anchor above
€390/year would cost nothing to add and might sell.

**Sequencing for the eventual info product.** Build the business to real revenue first, *then*
deconstruct it into a playbook. Doing both at once is too much work and you have no proof. Bonus:
turning your process into a teachable framework is a forcing function that systemises it —
otherwise the method stays fragmented in your head.

## 5. Jim — ex-Google SWE ($350K salary → $10K/month, 140K IG followers in 100 days)

**The single highest-converting growth mechanic in this batch: the SERIES.** Not daily posting —
daily posting *inside a numbered, finite series with a stated goal and a visible countdown*.
His: **"50 days to $1K"**, with 50 numbered post-its on the wall, one physically pulled off on
camera at the end of every video. It creates a "what happens tomorrow" loop, which is what
converts viewers into followers.
Comparables he cites: Khloe Shei, "30 lessons by 30" → over 1M followers. Cheetah Rajan,
documenting a luxury cabin build daily → 100K+ followers in ~70 days.
His own biggest mistake was **abandoning the series after day 50** — "I sort of just got lost."
Growth flattened and he never regained the straight line.
For DraftToDone the obvious framing already exists in the plan and isn't being used as a series:
*"90 days from €0 to 60 paying KDP operators"*, numbered from day 1, with the real tracking-sheet
numbers shown each day. It also solves what to post daily.

**The monetisation ladder that actually produced the $10K month — and it wasn't the low ticket.**
- $30 pre-sale of a course → a few copies
- $99 launch → refunded **everyone** out of money guilt, turned it into a free lead magnet
- $20 digital product → ~$2,000 total
- **$1,500 × 8 seats, 4-week cohort** = **$12,000**. One group call per week, one 1:1, async
  Discord access.

That last line is the entire $10K month. `[CONTRADICTS] the low-ticket-only path.` At €14.99/week
you need ~170 subscribers to clear €10K/month; eight people at €1,500 gets there in one email. And
the delivery *is* the known "talk to 50 customers" work — except paid, with the most committed
operators, producing the transcripts that rewrite the product. Founding-cohort seats at a real
price, not as an early-bird discount on the subscription.

**Money guilt is a named, expensive failure mode.** He refunded every buyer because he felt like a
scammer, then spent weeks unable to charge, which killed his series and his momentum. He underpriced
the $99 course and says he'd go higher today. Florian volunteers the same: launched at €79 a course
that should have been multiples of that, out of fear of asking for money. Both name it as their
top regret. Relevant to a founder currently at €14.99/week with two customers.

`[CONTRADICTS] X-first, again — with the cleanest natural experiment of the eight.` He spent a
month on X: **several hundred posts → 50 followers.** He then made **one** short-form video, posted
it to IG/TikTok/YouTube — it flopped on two and did **over 1M views on Instagram.** Fourth source
in this batch pointing the same direction. The relevant conclusion is not "X is bad" but that
**platform-format fit dominates effort**, and the plan's current 7:1 ratio of text posts to videos
is the inverse of what four of these guests found.

**Instagram-specific mechanic that breaks the Postbridge workflow.** Reel thumbnails matter — a
good thumbnail pulls traffic from the Explore page, and it's visible in the analytics as a distinct
source. Scheduling tools can't set custom thumbnails (API limitation), so he abandoned Postbridge
and posts to IG/TikTok/YouTube manually. Trade-off to make consciously: automation vs. Explore
distribution.

**Postbridge geo-routing — relevant if posting from outside the target market.** Florian, posting
from Indonesia, found his TikToks were being served to Indonesian audiences; routing through
Postbridge got him distribution in Canada/UK/US. If the ICP is US KDP operators and posting happens
from France, check whether the same geo-skew is happening before blaming the content.

**Retention trick:** cut B-roll every ~2 seconds. It does **not** have to relate to the words — it
conveys the emotion or adds context. Pure retention device.

**Workload triage between two formats.** Talking-head = low production, fast, use it when
time-constrained but you have something worth saying. B-roll storytelling = only when creative
energy is actually there. Do not attempt the high-production format on a low-energy day; ship the
talking head. His daily structure: talk in the morning → a few seconds of raw footage during the
day → a nightly reflection with the progress update.

**The daily-posting threshold, concretely.** Daily is for beginners: quantity is how you reach
quality, and the point is the identity shift ("I'm someone who shows up on camera"). **After
roughly 50 consecutive posts** the habit and the camera comfort are built, and from there he'd
rather ship one banger a week than seven mediocre pieces. He has missed days since and doesn't
think it matters post-threshold. That gives the 90-day cadence a defensible internal structure:
days 1–50 non-negotiable daily, days 51–90 quality-weighted.

**Expectation calibration, from Florian himself:** "I've been posting every single day for the past
40 days and nothing really happened. It feels like I'm wasting my time." That's the host of the
podcast, with an existing French audience, at day 40. Plan for the trough.

**Stack:** phone + CapCut, Apple Notes (ideas and scripts, syncs phone↔laptop), ChatGPT/Gemini for
writing, Stan Store for payments, Notion for lead magnets, Discord for the cohort, Typefully for X.
**Swipe file:** saved Instagram videos = the hook and idea bank.
**Content pillars:** exactly three (his: freedom, wealth, meaning). Everything posted must sit
inside one. Kills the daily "what do I post" problem.

## 6. Florian Darroman + Borja Beso — Distribute.io (~$10K MRR, 76 paid users)

**The unit economics are the finding.** 76 active paid users ≈ $10K MRR → **~$130 per user per
month.** DraftToDone at €14.99/week (~€65/mo) needs roughly double the customer count for the same
revenue, on a prosumer audience that is harder to reach. Worth sitting with before the pricing is
treated as settled.

**The LLM-ranking recipe they sell, which is also the recipe they run.** Per customer per day:
one keyword-targeted article auto-published to the blog → repurposed into social posts on every
platform → **one natural comment with a brand citation in a relevant subreddit, daily** → plus a
backlink exchange ring between all their own users. Their claim about which part moves the needle:
**the Reddit engine.** Result on Florian's own site: cited in a **Google AI Overview in under a
week**, and ranking #1 in ChatGPT/Grok for "best paid OpenClaw community."
Two things to take: the daily-Reddit-citation habit is corroborated by Jackie Chou (ep. 3) from a
completely different direction, and the **backlink exchange between users of the same tool** is a
mechanic DraftToDone could run manually across a handful of friendly KDP-adjacent sites.

**Build the growth hacks you run into the product itself.** "Every single day we try new techniques
on our own product, and every time we find one that works, we add it as a feature." The
distribution experiments become the roadmap. For DraftToDone: every KDP metadata/compliance trick
that works on Antoine's own showcase book becomes a feature.

**Co-founder split as an explicit division of genius zones.** Borja was already at $10K MRR solo
with paid ads and growth hacks, and specifically went looking for someone to own distribution +
being the face. In **one week** together they rebranded (Rebel Growth → Distribute.io), redid the
dashboard, added integrations, and ran beta testers. Borja's reason for wanting a partner: "I can't
do a podcast myself or edit a podcast, I just don't have the time." Corroborates episode 7's
co-founder funnel.

**Modest but real:** they set an arbitrary north-star number ($200K MRR by year end) purely as a
motivational anchor and say so openly.

## 7. Travis Fischer (transitive_bs) — Hacker Residency, ex-Microsoft/Yahoo, 2 exits

**The co-founder funnel, and the one question that decides it.** Treat finding a collaborator as a
marketing funnel where the product is you: (1) build in public about exactly what you're doing and
what you want; (2) write down your **hard constraints and soft constraints** explicitly, like
dating — "I need someone deeply technical," "I want to live in Bali and do the indie thing" vs.
"I want a billion-dollar company"; (3) run a **short, scoped project together** before committing —
it cuts through everything.
The decisive test: *"You have a critical meeting — a big customer, or Sequoia. You can't make it.
Would you be comfortable having this person go and represent you?"* If no, they're an employee or
an advisor, not a co-founder. Second signal: are you always the one leading and following up? The
best pairings are **convergent** — two people already working on similar things independently.
Context for why it matters: the **#1 cause of startup failure is no product-market fit; #2 is
co-founder/team breakup.**

**"Fake work" (Paul Graham), and the antidote.** Founders — especially technical ones — default to
the work they're comfortable with, which is almost never the highest-leverage work. "When I don't
know what to do, I'm in my code editor." The residency's intervention is embarrassingly literal:
walk over and say *"you've got Cursor open — what are you doing? You're supposed to be marketing."*
For a solo founder the equivalent is a written weekly commitment someone else can see.

**Subtraction, not addition.** Their core operating principle, taken from HF0: the best people
don't need a packed calendar, they need distractions removed. Their only fixed calendar item all
week is **Monday evening 2-minute demos**. It does two things: seeing peers' progress sets the pace,
and it drills concise pitching — which he names as **the #1 skill gap he sees in indie hackers.**

**Founder-market fit as the longevity variable.** If your honest answer to "why this?" is "because
I'll make money," you won't go the extra mile when it gets hard. He also names the failure mode of
building only for yourself: everyone ends up selling shovels to shovel salesmen (a thousand dev
tools). Antoine's KDP catalog history + ban is the founder-market fit — and the ICP being KDP
operators rather than indie hackers is, by this framing, correct.

**The sponsorship pitch, copyable verbatim.** They got Cognition (Devin), OpenRouter and Exa to
sponsor a residency that had no business model, and broke even. The framing: *"You already sponsor
hackathons — $10–20K for a day or two of exposure to exactly this audience. This is a 30-day
hackathon. You get content mentioning you, with you in the background, for 30 days."* Delivery: a
Notion one-pager with the announcement tweet (200K views) embedded as social proof, sent by cold DM
to devrel contacts. Applicable to DraftToDone: a 90-day public build with daily content is
sponsorable by AI/publishing/print-on-demand tools on exactly that logic.

**Ship the leanest version with no business model, then monetise retroactively.** They funded batch
one out of pocket, booked the villa, *then* went looking for sponsors. "A lot of people talk about
doing this type of thing and it never ends up happening."
Funnel numbers: **500 applications → 75 personal interviews (done by him) → 10 selected.**

**Seed-strapping, and where the money actually is.** Raise a small round for runway without
committing to venture scale. Only three funds ever catered to intentional indie founders:
**TinySeed, Indie.vc, Calm Fund — and Calm Fund shut down in 2025.** VCs structurally dislike
seed-strapping because their model needs one 10,000x in a hundred; be honest about it rather than
misrepresenting your intent. The blunt math: "I am probably one of those 98." Also worth knowing:
in the aqui-hire exit he had **12 months to the first real check and a 4-year incentive**; in the
bootstrapped exit he negotiated **no vesting and a two-week transition**, and calls the difference
night and day.

**Build in public as "increasing your surface area for luck."** His personal channel is not social
posts — it's **open-source breadcrumbs on GitHub**. People find him through the repos. A viable
non-social distribution channel: open-source a small KDP metadata/compliance utility and let it
carry attribution back to DraftToDone.

**The three problems he sees in every indie hacker:** loneliness / no peer network; picking the
wrong thing to work on (YC "tarpit ideas" — problems you get stuck in for years); and distribution
being hard because developers would rather add features.

## Sustainability guardrails for a solo founder

Concrete, from the episodes — not general advice.

- **Watch the hands and forearms specifically.** Ole's stop signal was every tendon in both arms
  inflamed to the point where he could not use them. Chronic pain of that kind is a nervous-system
  alarm state, not a tissue injury, and it does not resolve by pushing through. Daily recording,
  editing and typing for 90 days loads exactly those structures. Unexplained pain that persists past
  a week is the signal, not a nuisance.
- **The trap is that it's working.** "If you have something that works, it's actually much harder
  than something that doesn't work — your whole external world tells you to keep doing it." There is
  no natural stopping signal inside a channel that is performing. The stop has to be scheduled in
  advance, while it's still hypothetical.
- **Set the daily threshold at 50, then switch to quality.** Jim: daily posting matters for
  beginners because it produces the identity shift and the camera comfort; after ~50 consecutive
  posts he'd rather ship one strong piece a week than seven weak ones, and has missed days since
  without harm. Structure the 90 days as **days 1–50 non-negotiable daily, days 51–90
  quality-weighted**. This is a defensible rule, not a slackening.
- **Never let the format colonise the days it isn't on.** Ole's specific rule after burning out: vlog
  **one day a week**, not daily, because daily vlogging turns every hour into "could this be a vlog?"
  and imposes light/timing/location constraints on ordinary life. CADENCE.md already protects Sunday
  — that protection is the load-bearing part of the plan, not the soft part.
- **Keep the two low-energy formats ready and use them without guilt.** Jim's rule: talking-head is
  low production and fast — ship that when time or energy is short; save the B-roll storytelling for
  days when the creative energy is actually there. Attempting the high-production format on a bad day
  is how streaks break.
- **Build the asset that survives you stopping.** Ole's entire seven-figure identity was abandoned,
  and the **40,000-person email list survived it** — he kept selling old products with no new
  production. He funneled to email from week one. Everything in CADENCE.md that feeds the list and
  the SEO base is transferable; the personal posting cadence is not, and "for most personal brand
  stuff it's impossible to get someone else in — you have to kill it if you don't want to do it."
- **Apply the identity test before day 1, not after day 90.** Ole built a persona that worked, got
  called out by a follower as fake, and agreed instantly. The check: can you embody "the KDP
  operator guy" for five years without resenting it? He warns it's a one-way door — "four, five, ten
  years go by and you're still the SEO guy." The genuine version here (a banned operator who rebuilt
  the tooling) is embodiable; a generic AI-founder persona probably isn't.
- **Money guilt is the specific failure mode to pre-commit against.** Jim refunded every buyer of a
  $99 course out of a feeling of scamming people, then spent weeks unable to charge, which killed his
  series and his growth. Florian launched a large course at €79 for the same reason. Both name it as
  their top regret. Decide the founding-cohort price now, in writing, while it's abstract.
- **The exception that justifies overriding all of the above.** Jackie Chou went $70–80K → $500K per
  month in three months by dropping everything for one channel that had just started working.
  Sprinting is correct — but the trigger is **evidence that a channel is converting**, never a
  calendar date or a follower count. Everything else stays boring until then.
